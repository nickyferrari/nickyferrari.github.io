The Kok clock

Photosystem II splits water four electrons at a time, so its oxygen-evolving complex has to count to four. Fire a train of saturating flashes at dark-adapted cells and the oxygen comes out in a beautiful period-four oscillation, peaking on the third flash and damping away — the 1970 Kok result that told us the mechanism before anyone could see the structure. Set the miss probability to zero and the oscillation never damps — every center stays in lockstep and oxygen appears only on flashes 3, 7, 11. Misses are what smear the clock: a few percent per flash and the whole population has decohered into a flat steady state within twenty flashes.

Why the second component matters. The donor side counts to four, but the acceptor side (the QA/QB two-electron gate) counts to two, and a real measurement records their sum. Fit a period-four kinetic model straight to that sum and the optimizer reads the period-two ripple as fast damping, biasing the miss probability, the backward transitions, and the starting populations. In Ferrari, Russell & Vinyard (2026) I used the Empirical Wavelet Transform to pull the two apart before fitting, which recovers the donor-side transition probabilities with much less bias — including on Joliot and Forbush's original 1969 data. Switch "Show" to Separated components to see the two signals this demo added together.

The donor-side model here is the standard Kok scheme (misses, double hits, no backward transitions). The period-two trace is a schematic damped alternation standing in for the acceptor-side oscillation, not a full QA/QB simulation — it is here to show the superposition problem, not to fit your data.
